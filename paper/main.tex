\begin{abstract}
Language models are increasingly used to write descriptions of what an option would
mean for a particular person, which are then embedded and used as features in a
behavioural model. We ask what those descriptions actually contribute. Using a
three-stage pipeline -- an interpretable random-utility model, a shape-constrained
boosted residual, and a language channel combined with them at the probability level --
we separate three quantities that are usually reported as one: what \emph{any} second
predictor contributes, what the \emph{text} contributes, and what the \emph{reader
architecture} contributes. On three travel-mode datasets and one retail dataset,
evaluated with respondent-disjoint splits and out-of-fold calibration, the text is worth
\nats{0.029} nats on \dataset{LPMC} and nothing on \dataset{Optima}; a matched channel
containing no text attracts a \emph{larger} mixture weight than the text does; and an
ordinary two-layer network on the same embeddings matches or beats the designed reader
everywhere. We introduce a uniform-floor control, which replaces the language channel by the uniform
distribution at the same fitted weight and is strictly stronger than the shuffled,
identity-text and random-embedding controls in common use: on two of four datasets the
apparent gain survives those three and fails this one. Under the only time-respecting split
our data support, the effect disappears and a shuffled control matches it. Four proposed
improvements give no gain, and we report the diagnostics that explain why. An audit of the
generation inputs finds statistics derived from the respondent's own choices inside the
prompt, and $2.4$--$26.3\%$ of sentences asserting claims the evidence does not establish.
\end{abstract}
