\title{Outcome-Conditioned Utility:\\Identifying What Generated Consequences Contribute to Choice Models}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Discrete choice models are increasingly augmented with text that a language model writes
about what each alternative would mean for the individual facing the decision, on the
premise that the text supplies perceptual content the tabulated attributes omit. The
standard evidence for that premise is an improvement in held-out log-likelihood. We show
that this evidence is insufficient in three separate ways, and supply the procedure that
replaces it. First, a parameter-matched channel containing no text attracts a
\emph{larger} fitted mixture weight than the text channel does, so weight is not content.
Second, mixing any distribution into a sharp model bounds its log loss, and on two of our
four datasets that bound accounts for the entire apparent gain; we introduce a
weight-matched uninformative-channel control that isolates it, and show it is strictly
stronger than the shuffled, identity-text and random-embedding controls in use. Third, a
deterministic template built from exactly the recorded attributes predicts at least as
well as the generated text whenever the text channel is scored alone. We then give the
positive procedure: erase from the text representation every direction linearly
predictable from the alternative's attributes, its identity and the person covariates, so
that any surviving contribution cannot be a linear restatement of what the generator was
shown, and stack the mixture weight out of fold rather than setting it by hand. Under
respondent-disjoint evaluation an identified contribution survives on three of four
datasets -- $0.017$ nats on London travel choices, $0.009$ on Swiss stated-preference
tasks, $0.042$ on retail purchases -- and is indistinguishable from zero on Swiss reported
trips. What erasure costs varies sharply and is itself informative: it removes $43\%$ of
the London gain, none of the Swiss stated-preference gain, and \emph{improves} the retail
channel, which is what one expects where the generated text is largely restating a price
the attribute channel already has. Four proposed improvements to the text channel produce no
gain, and we give the diagnostics that explain each failure. We conclude that a claim that
generated text carries information beyond the attributes it was generated from requires
this procedure rather than a likelihood comparison.
\end{abstract}
