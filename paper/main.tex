\title{Outcome-Conditioned Utility:\\Identifying What Generated Consequences Contribute to Choice Models}

\author{Anonymous authors\\Paper under double-blind review}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Choice models are increasingly augmented with text that a language model writes about what
each alternative would mean for the individual facing the decision, on the premise that the
text supplies perceptual content the tabulated attributes omit. We specify this as a
random-utility model in which utility is composed over a generated consequence basis, and
estimate it on four choice datasets against nine reference specifications -- multinomial,
nested and mixed logit, an integrated choice and latent variable model, and five learned
utility specifications -- all fitted on identical respondent-disjoint partitions with the
same attributes and the same two history variables. The proposed specification attains the
lowest test log-loss on two of the three travel datasets, improving on the best reference by
$0.034$ nats on London trips and $0.028$ on Swiss reported trips, and is beaten by gradient
boosting by $0.034$ nats on Swiss stated-preference tasks.

Improvement in fit, however, does not establish that the text carries perceptual content, and
our central contribution is the procedure that does. We show three ways the usual evidence
fails: a parameter-matched channel containing no text attracts a \emph{larger} fitted mixture
weight than the text channel; mixing any distribution into a sharp model bounds its log loss,
which on two datasets accounts for the entire apparent gain; and a deterministic template
built from exactly the recorded attributes predicts at least as well as the generated text
whenever that channel is scored alone. We then erase from the text representation every
direction linearly predictable from the alternative's attributes, its identity and the person
covariates, so that any surviving contribution cannot be a linear restatement of what the
generator was shown. A contribution survives on three of four datasets -- $0.017$ nats on
London trips, $0.009$ on Swiss stated-preference tasks, $0.042$ on retail purchases -- and not
on Swiss reported trips. What erasure costs differs sharply and is itself informative: it
removes $43\%$ of the London gain, none of the Swiss stated-preference gain, and
\emph{improves} the retail channel, where the generated text is largely restating a price the
attribute channel already has. We conclude that a claim that generated text carries
information beyond the attributes it was generated from requires this procedure rather than a
likelihood comparison, and we report what remains once it is applied.
\end{abstract}
