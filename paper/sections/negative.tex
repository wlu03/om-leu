\section{Four proposals that did not work}
\label{sec:negative}

Each of the following was motivated by a diagnosis of why the language channel underperforms.
We report them because the diagnoses are testable and the failures are informative; \Cref{tab:improvements} in \Cref{app:extra} gives the four numbers.

\paragraph{Grounded generation.} If the generated text mixes supported outcomes with unverified
judgements, separating them should help. We wrote a prompt that tags every sentence as
\emph{observed}, \emph{derived} or \emph{unknown} and forbids invented quantities, affordability
judgements and demographic inferences, with programmatic validators for schema, evidence
vocabulary and numeral support. Regenerating under it needs a generation budget we did not have,
so the comparison is recorded as blocked rather than as a negative result. What we can report is
the audit of the existing generations (\Cref{sec:audit}) and the templated-facts control, which
predicts \emph{better} than the generated text whenever the channel is scored alone.

\paragraph{Consistency training.} If the representation tracks outcome magnitudes rather than
wording, then raising a fare should lower the financial score and leave the time score alone, and
a claim-preserving paraphrase should move nothing. We built an edit registry with units,
eligibility rules and declared affected and unaffected axes, and paraphrase families that
preserve quantities, negation polarity and evidence status, holding out families and edit
magnitudes for evaluation. Training on these objectives produced no predictive gain in any
configuration. More informative than the score: on held-out edit types the score moves in the
wrong direction on $25$--$43\%$ of eligible events, and the ordering loss barely changes that.
The representation is not behaviourally consistent, and making it more consistent did not make it
more accurate.

\paragraph{Complementarity-aware training.} If the language channel wastes capacity relearning
what the conventional model knows, training it against held-out numeric predictions should
redirect it. We fine-tune on
$-\log\!\big((1-\pi_{\text{train}}) p^{\text{num}}_{y} + \pi_{\text{train}} q_{y}\big)$
with the numeric side detached and produced by inner folds inside the outer training partition,
so the language channel never sees numeric predictions that used those labels. The gradient
identity
$\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{mix}} = \rho \cdot \nabla(-\log q_y)$ with
$\rho = \pi q_y / ((1-\pi)p_y + \pi q_y)$ is verified against autograd. The mixture did not
improve, and the standalone scores became worse, which is the trade the objective makes but not
the one we wanted.

\paragraph{Conditional mixture gate.} If the text helps in identifiable situations, the weight
should depend on context. A regularised logistic gate over history length, a missingness
indicator and choice-set size, fitted only on held-out channel predictions and shrunk towards the
global weight, spread the per-event weight from $0.15$ to $0.58$ and predicted no better than a
single number. The control that matters: the same gate over a channel with no text scored $0.009$
worse, so gate capacity is not a hidden source of gain.
\label{sec:gate}
For any claim about monotonicity of the complete pipeline, note that
$\partial p_j/\partial x = (1-\pi)\,\partial q_j/\partial x + \pi\,\partial r_j/\partial x +
(r_j - q_j)\,\partial \pi/\partial x$; a sign constraint inside the residual binds only the first
term.

\subsection{Why the personalised weights vanish inside the mixture}
\label{sec:why-weights}

The largest knock-out in \Cref{tab:knockouts} disappears when the channel is mixed, and the
explanation is measurable rather than rhetorical.

First, the personalisation is genuine: the learned weights vary across people (standard deviation
$0.23$ on the reliability head, range $0.92$, only $65\%$ of people sharing a top-weighted head),
and a variant that learns \emph{one} weight vector shared by everybody recovers none of the loss
($0.5626$ against $0.5628$ for fixed uniform weights and $0.5262$ for the personalised version),
with the shared vector converging to nearly flat. What the ablation removes is per-person
allocation, not the ability to learn which axes matter.

Second, the loss is a tail, not a shift. Sorting test events by how much the ablation hurts, the
worst tenth carries $+0.97$ on \dataset{Optima} and $+0.53$ on \dataset{LPMC}, while the
remaining nine tenths carry $+0.05$ and $-0.02$: on \dataset{LPMC} the ablated model is slightly
\emph{better} on ninety per cent of events. Those tail events are ones the conventional model
already ranks correctly ($72\%$ and $67\%$ respectively).

Third, \Cref{eq:floor} bounds what such a failure can cost inside the mixture, and the first-order
sensitivity of the mixture loss to the language channel is the standalone change multiplied by
$\rho = \pi q_y/p_y$, which collapses exactly where the ablated channel collapses. Measured
attenuation: $25.9\times$ on \dataset{Optima}, $21.9\times$ on \dataset{LPMC}.

The design implication is specific. The personalised weights raise average accuracy without
changing \emph{which} events the language channel wins -- its win rate against the conventional
channel is $36.2\%$ before the ablation and $36.1\%$ after. A component that improves accuracy
without moving that set is what a probability mixture discards.

\subsection{Transfer and sample size}

A scorer trained on one dataset and frozen is worse than fitting on the target in all six ordered
directions; fine-tuning recovers most of the difference, which says the transferred parameters are
a reasonable initialisation rather than a transferable notion of outcome level. Learning curves
that subsample whole households show templated facts leading generated text at every training
size, with the gap narrowing as data grows. Both tables are in \Cref{app:extra}.
