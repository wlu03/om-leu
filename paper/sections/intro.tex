\section{Introduction}

A choice model predicts which alternative a person selects from an available set. A growing
body of work adds to such a model a channel that reads text: a language model is asked what
each alternative would mean for this person, the answer is embedded by a frozen encoder, and
a small network reads the embedding. The motivation is that people choose on perceived
consequences rather than on tabulated attributes, and that the tabulated attributes do not
encode those consequences. The evidence offered is almost always the same: the combined
system attains a better held-out log-likelihood than the model without the text.

That evidence does not establish the premise. Three mechanisms produce the same measurement,
and they call for different conclusions.

\textbf{Combination.} Pooling two predictive distributions improves a proper score whenever
their errors differ, whatever the second one reads. Worse, the improvement is partly
guaranteed: since $p = (1-\pi) q + \pi r \geq (1-\pi) q$, the log loss of the pool exceeds
that of the base model by at most $-\log(1-\pi)$ on any event, so a channel that predicts at
chance still improves a base model that is confidently wrong somewhere.

\textbf{Restatement.} The generator is shown the attributes. Text derived from them may
carry nothing else, in which case the text channel is a re-encoding of columns the base model
already has.

\textbf{Content.} The text carries something the attributes do not.

Only the third supports the premise, and separating it from the first two requires controls
that the literature on this family of models does not run. We supply them, and we supply the
positive procedure that makes a content claim identifiable rather than asserted.

\paragraph{What we do.} We hold one representative pipeline fixed -- a random-utility model, a
shape-constrained residual, and a text channel pooled with them -- and vary one component at a
time. The pipeline is not our contribution; holding it fixed is what makes the comparisons
clean. Every variant sees the same text, encoder, base model and ensembling budget.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The fitted mixture weight is not a measure of content. The text channel receives
      $\pi = 0.21$--$0.40$ across datasets; a parameter-matched channel containing \emph{no}
      text receives $0.37$--$0.52$ (\Cref{tab:pi}).
\item A weight-matched uninformative-channel control separates the bound above from
      information. On two of four datasets the entire apparent gain is the bound
      (\Cref{tab:floor}), and this is invisible to the shuffled, identity-text and
      random-embedding controls in use, because each of those is itself a trained model
      producing a non-uniform distribution.
\item A deterministic template containing exactly the recorded quantities predicts at least
      as well as the generated text whenever the channel is scored alone (\Cref{tab:main}).
\item After erasing from the text representation every direction linearly predictable from
      the alternative's attributes, its identity and the person covariates, a contribution
      survives on three of four datasets and not on the fourth
      (\Cref{tab:identification}). What erasure costs differs sharply between them and is
      itself informative.
\item No designed reader beats an ordinary two-layer network given the same information
      (\Cref{tab:structural}), and four proposed improvements produce no gain
      (\Cref{sec:negative}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope of the claim.} Erasure is linear, so ``not a restatement'' means not
linearly recoverable from the conditioning set; exhaustive nonlinear erasure is not available
\citep{ravfogel2022kernelized}. We do not use the word \emph{mediated}: mediation carries an
identification assumption \citep{imai2010general} that none of our designs tests. We say
\emph{outcome-conditioned}, and we state in \Cref{sec:discussion} what that does and does not
license.
