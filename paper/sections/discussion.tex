\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.} On one of four datasets the generated text carries a
contribution that survives every control we could construct, including erasure of the
attribute span and a weight-matched uninformative channel: $0.017$ nats on \dataset{LPMC},
interval excluding zero. On \dataset{Optima} there is a gain in the pooled model and no
identified content behind it. On \dataset{Swissmetro} and \dataset{Amazon} the pooled gain is
the bound of \Cref{eq:floor}. The honest summary is that generated consequence text can carry
choice-relevant information beyond the attributes it was generated from, that this is the
exception rather than the rule across our datasets, and that the difference between the
datasets is visible only with the procedure of \Cref{sec:identification}.

\paragraph{What it does not support.} No causal identification: nothing tests the sequential
ignorability that \emph{mediation} would require \citep{imai2010general}, which is why we avoid
the word. No psychological identification: the axes are prompt labels and the weights fitted
scalars, and tying them to what people attend to needs elicitation from people. No
architectural claim, no equivalence from intervals containing zero, and the identification is
linear in scope.

\paragraph{Three reporting recommendations.} Report the fitted weight beside the weight a
text-free channel attracts; include a weight-matched uninformative control, since the usual
controls are trained models and miss \Cref{eq:floor}; and state which split a number comes
from, since the two defensible splits disagree here in direction, not magnitude.

\paragraph{Limitations and the design that would settle it.} The datasets are public case
studies of a few hundred to a thousand respondents, two experimental arms are incomplete, and
the identification is linear in scope. \Cref{app:limits} states each, and describes the two
designs -- an exclusion restriction on the generator's inputs, and elicitation from people --
that would convert a conditional predictive claim into a claim about perception.
